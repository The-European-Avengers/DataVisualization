\section{Background and Motivation}
\label{sec:background}

Housing affordability is one of the most immediate economic questions facing
anyone who is about to finish a degree and settle somewhere. As a group of
master's students in Denmark, the choice of where to live after graduation is
not an abstract problem: it determines which part of the country is realistic
to build a life in, and it is usually made on the basis of anecdote rather than
evidence. This is what drew us to the topic. We wanted to replace the common
impressions about Danish housing, that it has become unaffordable, that
Copenhagen is a special case, that the provinces are cheap, with something we
could actually examine.

The public discussion of Danish housing is normally conducted in terms of the
national average price, or the year-on-year change in it. That framing is
convenient but it conceals the two things that matter most to the person making
the decision. The first is \emph{where}: a national average cannot distinguish a
market that has quadrupled from one that has merely kept pace with incomes, and
it is precisely that difference which determines whether moving is worthwhile.
The second is \emph{who}: a price is only meaningful relative to the income of
the household that has to carry it, and a single person and a couple face the
same price with very different means. Neither dimension survives aggregation
into a single national figure, and both are central to the questions we set out
in Section~\ref{sec:objectives}.

Denmark is an unusually good place to ask these questions, because the relevant
registers are public, long-running and consistently defined. Statistics Denmark
publishes property transactions and family incomes on a compatible regional
breakdown, the eleven \emph{landsdele}, going back to 1995, which makes it
possible to put prices and incomes on the same footing over three decades rather
than comparing them indirectly. A preliminary inspection of the combined data,
described in Section~\ref{sec:data}, confirmed that the aggregate picture is
indeed hiding the interesting structure. In 1995 the price of a one-family house
ranged from roughly $2.3$ to $6.3$ times the average family's annual disposable
income depending on the landsdel; by 2024 the same measure ranged from about
$3.5$ to $15.2$. The gap between the most and least affordable part of the
country, in other words, widened considerably over the period, which is not
something that can be read off a national series at all.

This combination of a spatial dimension, a long time dimension and several
household and property categories is what makes the problem a visualisation
problem rather than a table. Three interacting dimensions cannot be presented
convincingly as numbers on a page: the regional pattern asks for a map, the
thirty-year development asks for time series and for animation to make the
divergence visible as it happens, and the comparison between household types
asks for small multiples that hold the price constant while the income changes.
Our own background in software and data engineering made this an attractive
project for a further reason, namely that it required building the whole
pipeline, from the raw register exports through the joining and cleaning
described in Section~\ref{sec:data} to an interactive dashboard, rather than
visualising a dataset that had already been prepared for us.
